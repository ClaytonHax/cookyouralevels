# Dynamics
> Examiners want Newton's laws and momentum conservation applied with careful signs and with the reasoning (not just the answer) stated.

## Key ideas

### Newton's three laws
1. A body stays at rest or moves with constant velocity unless a resultant force acts on it.
2. The resultant force on a body is equal to the rate of change of its momentum, and acts in the direction of the change. For constant mass this is $F = ma$.
3. If body A exerts a force on body B, then B exerts a force on A that is equal in magnitude, opposite in direction, of the same type, and acts on a different body. The two forces of a third-law pair never act on the same object, so they never cancel.

### Mass, weight and momentum
Mass is a measure of the inertia of a body (its resistance to a change in velocity); it is a scalar and the same everywhere. Weight is the gravitational force on a body, $W = mg$, a vector that depends on the field strength. Linear momentum is $p = mv$, a vector. Newton's second law in its general form is $F = \Delta p / \Delta t$.

### Drag and terminal velocity
A body falling through a fluid feels weight downward, and drag (and upthrust) upward. Drag increases with speed. At first the resultant is downward and the body accelerates; as drag grows the resultant, and so the acceleration, shrinks. At terminal velocity the resultant force is zero (weight = drag + upthrust), so the velocity is constant. The velocity-time graph is a curve with decreasing gradient levelling to a horizontal line. A greater cross-sectional area or a more viscous fluid lowers the terminal velocity. Opening a parachute increases drag abruptly, giving a deceleration to a new, lower terminal velocity.

### Conservation of momentum
In a closed (isolated) system, total momentum before an interaction equals total momentum after, because the internal forces are equal and opposite (Newton's third law) and act for the same time. In two dimensions, momentum is conserved separately in two perpendicular directions; use components, or a closed vector triangle. For an explosion from rest, total momentum remains zero, so the fragments move off in opposite directions with equal and opposite momenta.

### Elastic and inelastic collisions
In an elastic collision kinetic energy is conserved; the relative speed of approach equals the relative speed of separation. In an inelastic collision some kinetic energy is transferred to other forms (thermal, sound, deformation), though total energy and momentum are still conserved. A perfectly inelastic collision is one where the bodies stick together.

### Impulse
Impulse is force multiplied by the time for which it acts, and equals the change in momentum: $F\Delta t = \Delta p$. On a force-time graph, the area under the curve is the impulse. A longer contact time lowers the average force for the same change in momentum (crumple zones, airbags).

## Definitions to learn
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Mass | A measure of the inertia of a body; its resistance to change in velocity |
| Weight | The force on a body due to a gravitational field |
| Linear momentum | The product of mass and velocity |
| Newton's second law | The resultant force is proportional to the rate of change of momentum and acts in the same direction |
| Impulse | The product of force and the time for which it acts, equal to the change in momentum |
| Principle of conservation of momentum | The total momentum of a system of interacting bodies remains constant provided no external resultant force acts |
| Elastic collision | A collision in which total kinetic energy is conserved |
| Terminal velocity | The constant velocity reached when the resultant force on the body is zero |

## Worked example
A ball of mass $0.16\ \text{kg}$ hits a wall at $25\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ and rebounds at $15\ \text{m s}^{-1}$, contact lasting $0.020\ \text{s}$. Take the rebound direction as positive.

$$\begin{aligned}
\Delta p &= 0.16\,(15 - (-25)) = 6.4\ \text{N s} \\
F &= \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = \frac{6.4}{0.020} = 3.2\times10^{2}\ \text{N}
\end{aligned}$$

A $2.0\ \text{kg}$ trolley at $3.0\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ collides with a stationary $1.0\ \text{kg}$ trolley and they stick together.

$$\begin{aligned}
2.0(3.0) &= (2.0 + 1.0)\,v \;\Rightarrow\; v = 2.0\ \text{m s}^{-1} \\
E_{k,\text{before}} &= \tfrac12(2.0)(3.0)^2 = 9.0\ \text{J}, \qquad E_{k,\text{after}} = \tfrac12(3.0)(2.0)^2 = 6.0\ \text{J}
\end{aligned}$$

Kinetic energy has decreased by $3.0\ \text{J}$, so the collision is inelastic.

## Common mistakes
- Treating the weight and the normal contact force as a third-law pair (they act on the same body and are different types).
- Forgetting that momentum is a vector (ignoring the sign on rebound).
- Claiming momentum is not conserved in an inelastic collision.
- Saying that at terminal velocity "there are no forces"; the resultant is zero.
- Stating "$F = ma$" when mass changes; use $F = \Delta p/\Delta t$.
- Confusing mass and weight units.

## Exam tips
- For conservation of momentum, say "no external resultant force" explicitly.
- To prove a collision is elastic or inelastic, calculate kinetic energy before and after.
- Impulse-based force answers are average forces; say so.
- Use full sentences for terminal velocity: weight balances drag, so zero resultant force, so zero acceleration.
- Always draw a before-and-after diagram with a labelled positive direction.

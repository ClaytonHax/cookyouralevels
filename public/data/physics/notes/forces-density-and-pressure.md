# Forces, density and pressure
> Examiners want clear free-body diagrams, precise definitions, and moments and equilibrium argued from first principles.

## Key ideas

### Types of force
- Gravitational: attraction between masses; acts at the centre of gravity; the weight of an object.
- Electric: acts on charged particles, in the direction of the field for positive charges.
- Magnetic: acts on moving charges and current-carrying wires in a magnetic field.
- Upthrust: upward force on an object in a fluid, caused by the pressure difference between its lower and upper surfaces.
- Friction: contact force opposing relative motion (or its tendency) between surfaces.
- Drag: resistive force on a body moving through a fluid; grows with speed.
- Tension: pulling force along a rope, string or wire.
- Normal contact force: perpendicular reaction force from a surface, caused by electrostatic repulsion between atoms when the surface is slightly deformed.

A free-body diagram shows one object only, with every force on it drawn as an arrow from the object (or its centre of gravity), labelled with name and direction.

### Density
Density is mass per unit volume, $\rho = m/V$. Compare materials by measuring mass with a balance and volume by dimensions or by displacement.

### Pressure
Pressure is normal force per unit area, $p = F/A$. Smaller area gives larger pressure for the same force. In a liquid of uniform density, the pressure due to the liquid at depth $h$ is $p = \rho g h$. It arises because the column of liquid above has weight $\rho g h A$ acting on area $A$. Pressure increases linearly with depth and acts equally in all directions at a point. The total pressure at depth also includes the pressure at the surface (for example atmospheric).

### Upthrust
The pressure at the bottom of a submerged object is greater than at the top, so there is a net upward force. For an object fully immersed this equals the weight of the fluid displaced, $F = \rho_f g V$, where $V$ is the volume of fluid displaced. An object floats when upthrust equals its weight.

### Moments, couples and torque
The moment of a force about a point is force times perpendicular distance from the point to the line of action of the force. A couple is a pair of equal, parallel, opposite forces not acting along the same line; it produces rotation without translation. Torque of a couple is one force times the perpendicular distance between the lines of action. The principle of moments says that for a body in equilibrium the sum of clockwise moments about any point equals the sum of anticlockwise moments about that point.

### Equilibrium
For a body in equilibrium, there is no resultant force (so no linear acceleration) and no resultant moment (so no angular acceleration). Three coplanar forces in equilibrium form a closed triangle when drawn head to tail. Resolving forces into perpendicular components is often easier than drawing the triangle. Taking moments about the point where an unknown force acts removes that force from the equation.

## Definitions to learn
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Density | Mass per unit volume |
| Pressure | Normal force acting per unit cross-sectional area |
| Upthrust | The net upward force on an object in a fluid, equal to the weight of the fluid displaced |
| Moment of a force | The product of the force and the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force |
| Couple | A pair of equal and opposite parallel forces whose lines of action do not coincide |
| Torque of a couple | The product of one of the forces and the perpendicular distance between the forces |
| Principle of moments | For a body in equilibrium, the sum of the clockwise moments about any point equals the sum of the anticlockwise moments about that point |
| Centre of gravity | The point at which the whole weight of a body may be considered to act |

## Worked example
Pressure due to sea water ($\rho = 1030\ \text{kg m}^{-3}$) at $30\ \text{m}$ depth:

$$p = \rho g h = 1030 \times 9.81 \times 30 = 3.03\times10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$$

A uniform plank of length $3.0\ \text{m}$ and weight $120\ \text{N}$ rests on supports at each end. A $80\ \text{N}$ load sits $1.0\ \text{m}$ from the left end. Moments about the left support:

$$\begin{aligned}
R_R \times 3.0 &= 120 \times 1.5 + 80 \times 1.0 = 260 \\
R_R &= 86.7\ \text{N} \\
R_L &= (120 + 80) - 86.7 = 113.3\ \text{N}
\end{aligned}$$

Upthrust on a block of volume $2.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3$ fully in water ($1000\ \text{kg m}^{-3}$): $F = 1000 \times 9.81 \times 2.0\times10^{-3} = 19.6\ \text{N}$.

## Common mistakes
- Using the length along a rod instead of the perpendicular distance in a moment.
- Forgetting to add atmospheric pressure when absolute pressure is asked.
- Using the density of the object, not the fluid, in $\rho g V$.
- Drawing forces on the ground or on other bodies in a free-body diagram.
- Describing a couple as "a single force that rotates"; it has zero resultant force.
- Calling the weight and the normal force a third-law pair.

## Exam tips
- Choose the pivot so the largest number of unknowns have zero moment.
- Write both conditions for equilibrium separately in your answer.
- For upthrust explanations, always mention pressure difference between top and bottom.
- Label every arrow in a free-body diagram; unlabelled arrows lose marks.
- Convert units (cm to m, g to kg) before substituting.

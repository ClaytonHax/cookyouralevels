# Particle physics
> Examiners want conservation laws applied to nuclear equations and decays, with quark-level explanations of beta decay.

## Key ideas

### The nuclear atom and alpha-particle scattering
In the Geiger-Marsden experiment a narrow beam of alpha particles in a vacuum was directed at thin gold foil, with a detector (a scintillating screen) moved around it. Observations and conclusions:
- Most alpha particles passed straight through, so the atom is mostly empty space.
- A small fraction was deflected through large angles, and a very few (roughly 1 in 8000) rebounded backwards, so there is a small, dense, positively charged nucleus containing most of the mass; the large deflections come from electrostatic repulsion.
The nucleus has a radius of order $10^{-15}\ \text{m}$, compared to about $10^{-10}\ \text{m}$ for the atom. The experiment is in a vacuum because alpha particles are absorbed by air.

### Nuclear composition and isotopes
The nucleus contains protons (charge $+e$) and neutrons (no charge), together called nucleons; electrons orbit outside. The proton number $Z$ is the number of protons; the nucleon (mass) number $A$ is protons plus neutrons; neutron number is $A - Z$. A nuclide is written ${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$. Isotopes of an element have the same $Z$ but different $A$, so the same chemical properties but different nuclear properties.

### Nuclear equations
In every nuclear reaction charge (proton number) and nucleon number are conserved, and so is total mass-energy (small mass differences appear as energy released, $E = mc^2$). Check that top numbers and bottom numbers balance on each side.

### Alpha, beta and gamma decay
- Alpha decay: the nucleus emits an alpha particle (helium nucleus, ${}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$). $A$ falls by 4 and $Z$ by 2. Alpha particles are strongly ionising and have a short range in air, stopped by paper.
- Beta-minus decay: a neutron changes into a proton, emitting an electron and an electron antineutrino. $Z$ rises by 1, $A$ is unchanged.
- Beta-plus decay: a proton changes into a neutron, emitting a positron and an electron neutrino. $Z$ falls by 1, $A$ unchanged.
- Gamma emission: an excited nucleus emits a photon of electromagnetic radiation, with no change in $A$ or $Z$. It is weakly ionising and very penetrating.
Beta particles have a continuous spread of energies, which led to the neutrino being proposed so that energy and momentum were conserved.

### Quarks and hadrons
Hadrons feel the strong interaction and are made of quarks. The up (u) quark has charge $+\tfrac23 e$, the down (d) and strange (s) quarks have charge $-\tfrac13 e$; each has baryon number $\tfrac13$, and antiquarks have opposite charge. A baryon (such as the proton, uud, and neutron, udd) consists of three quarks, baryon number 1. A meson consists of a quark and an antiquark, baryon number 0. Charge check: proton $\tfrac23 + \tfrac23 - \tfrac13 = +1$; neutron $\tfrac23 - \tfrac13 - \tfrac13 = 0$.

### Leptons and beta decay at quark level
Leptons (electron, muon, tau, and their neutrinos) are fundamental particles that do not feel the strong interaction. Hadrons are not fundamental; they are made of quarks. In beta-minus decay a down quark changes to an up quark:

$$\text{d} \rightarrow \text{u} + \text{e}^- + \bar{\nu}_e$$

In beta-plus decay an up quark changes to a down quark:

$$\text{u} \rightarrow \text{d} + \text{e}^+ + \nu_e$$

Lepton number is conserved: the electron (+1) is balanced by the antineutrino (-1).

## Definitions to learn
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Nucleon | A proton or neutron in the nucleus |
| Proton number | The number of protons in the nucleus |
| Nucleon number | The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus |
| Isotopes | Nuclei of the same element with the same proton number but different numbers of neutrons |
| Hadron | A particle that experiences the strong interaction and is made of quarks |
| Baryon | A hadron made of three quarks |
| Meson | A hadron made of a quark and an antiquark |
| Lepton | A fundamental particle that does not experience the strong interaction |

## Worked example
Uranium-238 emits an alpha particle:

$$ {}^{238}_{92}\text{U} \rightarrow {}^{234}_{90}\text{Th} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$$

Check: nucleon number $238 = 234 + 4$; proton number $92 = 90 + 2$.

Strontium-90 ($Z = 38$) undergoes beta-minus decay:

$$ {}^{90}_{38}\text{Sr} \rightarrow {}^{90}_{39}\text{Y} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} + \bar{\nu}_e$$

Check: $A$: $90 = 90 + 0 + 0$; charge: $38 = 39 - 1 + 0$.

Neutron to proton at quark level:

$$\underbrace{\text{udd}}_{\text{neutron}} \rightarrow \underbrace{\text{uud}}_{\text{proton}} + \text{e}^- + \bar{\nu}_e$$

The charge on the left is $0$; on the right, $+1 - 1 + 0 = 0$.

## Common mistakes
- Saying the nucleus is "negatively charged" or that alphas are repelled by electrons.
- Writing the beta particle with nucleon number 1.
- Forgetting the (anti)neutrino, or using the neutrino for beta-minus instead of the antineutrino.
- Stating that gamma emission changes the element.
- Saying beta decay is a proton becoming a neutron (that is beta-plus).
- Counting the electron's charge in the nucleus or confusing proton number with nucleon number.

## Exam tips
- In scattering questions, link each observation to its conclusion in separate points.
- Write the nuclear equation by balancing top and bottom numbers independently.
- For quark-model charge, show the arithmetic of the three fractions.
- Name the particle changed at the quark level, not just "the nucleus".
- Quote that the strong interaction acts on hadrons only, not on leptons.

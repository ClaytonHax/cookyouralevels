# Waves
> Examiners want precise wave terms, correct use of $v = f\lambda$, intensity relationships, and careful reading of displacement graphs.

## Key ideas

### Progressive waves
A progressive wave transfers energy from one place to another without a net transfer of matter; the particles (or fields) oscillate about fixed positions. Key terms: displacement (distance of a point from its equilibrium position, in a stated direction), amplitude (maximum displacement), wavelength (minimum distance between two points oscillating in phase), period (time for one oscillation), frequency (oscillations per unit time) and wave speed (distance travelled by the wave profile per unit time). Phase difference is the fraction of a cycle by which one oscillation is ahead of another, measured in degrees or radians; points a distance $\Delta x$ apart differ in phase by $2\pi \Delta x/\lambda$.

### Wave speed and intensity
$v = f\lambda$, with $f = 1/T$. Intensity is the power transmitted per unit area perpendicular to the direction of travel, $I = P/A$ (unit $\text{W m}^{-2}$). Intensity is proportional to the square of amplitude, $I \propto A^2$. For a point source radiating uniformly, $I \propto 1/r^2$, because the same power spreads over a sphere of area $4\pi r^2$.

### Transverse and longitudinal waves; polarisation
In a transverse wave the oscillations are perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer (light, all electromagnetic waves, waves on a string). In a longitudinal wave the oscillations are parallel to it (sound), forming compressions and rarefactions. Only transverse waves can be polarised: a polarised wave oscillates in just one plane. Unpolarised light passing through a polariser emerges plane-polarised with half the intensity. If plane-polarised light of intensity $I_0$ meets a second polariser (analyser) whose axis makes an angle $\theta$ with its plane, the transmitted intensity is $I = I_0\cos^2\theta$. Rotating the analyser through $360^\circ$ gives two maxima and two zeros.

### Electromagnetic spectrum
Electromagnetic waves are oscillating electric and magnetic fields, transverse, and all travel at $c = 3.00\times10^{8}\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ in a vacuum. Approximate wavelength ranges, from long to short:
- radio waves: above about $10^{-1}\ \text{m}$
- microwaves: about $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-1}\ \text{m}$
- infrared: about $7\times10^{-7}$ to $10^{-3}\ \text{m}$
- visible: about $4\times10^{-7}$ to $7\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}$ (violet to red)
- ultraviolet: about $10^{-8}$ to $4\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}$
- X-rays: about $10^{-13}$ to $10^{-8}\ \text{m}$
- gamma rays: below about $10^{-11}\ \text{m}$ (overlaps X-rays)

### Doppler effect
When a source of sound moves relative to the observer, the observed frequency differs from the emitted frequency. For a moving source and stationary observer:

$$f_o = \frac{f_s v}{v \pm v_s}$$

Use minus when the source moves toward the observer (wavefronts bunched, higher frequency) and plus when it moves away (lower frequency).

### Graphs
A displacement-distance graph is a snapshot at one instant; read wavelength from peak to peak. A displacement-time graph shows the motion of a single point; read period from peak to peak. Amplitude is the maximum height from the equilibrium line. Wave speed needs both graphs (or $\lambda$ and $T$): $v = \lambda/T$.

## Definitions to learn
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Displacement | Distance of a point on the wave from its equilibrium position, in a specified direction |
| Amplitude | Maximum displacement of a point on the wave from its equilibrium position |
| Wavelength | Minimum distance between two points on a wave that are oscillating in phase |
| Period | Time taken for one complete oscillation of a point on the wave |
| Frequency | Number of oscillations per unit time at a point on the wave |
| Wave speed | The distance moved by the wave profile per unit time |
| Phase difference | The fraction of a cycle (as an angle) by which one oscillation leads or lags another |
| Intensity | Radiant power transmitted per unit cross-sectional area, perpendicular to the direction of travel |
| Polarised wave | A transverse wave in which the oscillations are in only one plane |

## Worked example
An ambulance siren emits $700\ \text{Hz}$ and the ambulance travels at $25\ \text{m s}^{-1}$. Take the speed of sound as $340\ \text{m s}^{-1}$.

$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Approaching: } f_o &= \frac{700 \times 340}{340 - 25} = 7.6\times10^{2}\ \text{Hz} \quad (756\ \text{Hz}) \\
\text{Receding: } f_o &= \frac{700 \times 340}{340 + 25} = 6.5\times10^{2}\ \text{Hz} \quad (652\ \text{Hz})
\end{aligned}$$

Plane-polarised light of intensity $I_0$ meets an analyser at $60^\circ$:

$$I = I_0\cos^2 60^\circ = 0.25\,I_0$$

A $440\ \text{Hz}$ note in air: $\lambda = v/f = 340/440 = 0.77\ \text{m}$.

## Common mistakes
- Defining wavelength as "length of one wave" without reference to in-phase points.
- Saying that a progressive wave carries matter.
- Applying polarisation to sound.
- Using amplitude where intensity is asked (the ratio is squared).
- Reading wavelength from a displacement-time graph or period from a displacement-distance graph.
- Choosing the wrong sign in the Doppler formula.

## Exam tips
- Intensity doubling the amplitude gives four times the intensity; halving amplitude gives one quarter.
- For polarisation, state that light is transverse as the reason it can be polarised.
- Write the Doppler denominator by reasoning (approaching means higher pitch) rather than memory.
- Quote approximate EM wavelength orders of magnitude, not exact numbers.
- Remember that the speed of all EM waves in a vacuum is the same; frequency and wavelength vary inversely.

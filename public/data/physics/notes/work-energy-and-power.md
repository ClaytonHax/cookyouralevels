# Work, energy and power
> Examiners want energy conservation applied step by step, with correct treatment of angles, dissipation and efficiency.

## Key ideas

### Work done
Work is the energy transferred when a force moves its point of application. For a constant force $F$ moving a body a displacement $s$ at angle $\theta$ to the force:

$$W = Fs\cos\theta$$

Only the component of force along the displacement does work; a force perpendicular to the motion (such as the centripetal force on a satellite in a circular orbit) does none. For a gas at constant pressure $p$ expanding by $\Delta V$, the work done by the gas is $W = p\Delta V$. Work done on a gas in compression has the same form.

### Kinetic and gravitational potential energy
Kinetic energy is the energy a body has because of its motion, $E_k = \tfrac12 mv^2$. Derived from $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$ with $F = ma$: the work done by the resultant force equals the change in kinetic energy. Gravitational potential energy is the energy a body has because of its position in a gravitational field; near the Earth's surface the change when lifted through height $\Delta h$ is $\Delta E_p = mg\Delta h$.

### Conservation of energy and dissipation
Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred from one form to another. In a mechanical system with no dissipative forces, the sum of kinetic and potential energy stays constant. When friction or drag act, some energy is transferred to thermal energy of the surroundings (dissipated), so mechanical energy falls but total energy is conserved. State this idea whenever a question asks "where has the energy gone?".

### Power
Power is the rate of doing work (or transferring energy), $P = W/t$. For a force moving a body at constant velocity $v$ along the line of the force, $P = Fv$. At constant speed on a level road, driving force equals resistive force, so engine power equals resistive force times speed.

### Efficiency
Efficiency is the fraction of the input energy (or power) that is transferred to a useful form:

$$\text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{useful output energy (or power)}}{\text{total input energy (or power)}}$$

It is quoted as a ratio or a percentage and can never exceed 100%.

## Definitions to learn
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Work done by a force | The product of the force and the displacement in the direction of the force |
| Joule | The work done when a force of 1 N moves its point of application 1 m in the direction of the force |
| Kinetic energy | The energy a body has due to its motion |
| Gravitational potential energy | The energy a body has due to its position in a gravitational field |
| Principle of conservation of energy | Energy cannot be created or destroyed; it can only be transferred from one form to another, so the total energy of a closed system is constant |
| Power | The rate of work done (or the rate of energy transfer) |
| Watt | A rate of energy transfer of one joule per second |
| Efficiency | The ratio of useful output energy (or power) to total input energy (or power) |

## Worked example
A crate is pulled $8.0\ \text{m}$ along level ground by a rope with tension $60\ \text{N}$ at $35^\circ$ above the horizontal.

$$W = Fs\cos\theta = 60 \times 8.0 \times \cos 35^\circ = 3.9\times10^{2}\ \text{J}$$

A ball is dropped from rest from $5.0\ \text{m}$ (no air resistance). Equate lost gravitational potential energy to gained kinetic energy:

$$\begin{aligned}
mgh &= \tfrac12 mv^2 \\
v &= \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2 \times 9.81 \times 5.0} = 9.9\ \text{m s}^{-1}
\end{aligned}$$

A motor raises a $50\ \text{kg}$ load through $12\ \text{m}$ in $20\ \text{s}$ with electrical input power $1.0\ \text{kW}$:

$$\begin{aligned}
\text{useful work} &= mg\Delta h = 50 \times 9.81 \times 12 = 5886\ \text{J} \\
\text{useful power} &= \frac{5886}{20} = 294\ \text{W} \\
\text{efficiency} &= \frac{294}{1000} = 0.29 \ (29\%)
\end{aligned}$$

## Common mistakes
- Using $\sin\theta$ instead of $\cos\theta$ when $\theta$ is measured from the displacement direction.
- Using the length of a slope instead of the vertical height in $mg\Delta h$.
- Stating that energy is "lost" instead of "transferred to thermal energy".
- Forgetting that power $Fv$ uses the force along the velocity.
- Computing efficiency as output divided by output, or giving it above 100%.
- Using $p\Delta V$ with volume in $\text{cm}^3$.

## Exam tips
- Start energy problems by listing energy forms at the start and end.
- If a problem involves friction, either use work done against friction ($Fs$) as an energy loss or use the work-energy theorem with the resultant force.
- For $P = Fv$ at constant speed, the driving force equals the sum of resistive forces.
- Give units with every line of working and keep two or three significant figures.
- Define terms in words with "per" or "rate", not as formulae alone, when the question says "define".
